## Pick a Starting Trade That Matches Your Risk Tolerance

The game does not ask you to pick a theme — it asks you to pick which kind of trouble you want to monetize. Gunsmith, chemist, water merchant, fence, farmer, and the "absolute rock bottom" start each carry a different profit curve and a different failure mode. Choosing badly here is the most common reason new players stall before the second rent payment.

| Starting path | Profit ceiling | Primary threat | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gunsmith | High | Inspections and permit checks | Players who enjoy paperwork and high-margin sales |
| Chemist | High | Addiction liability and guarded recipes | Long-term planners who protect their production chain |
| Water merchant | Steady, modest | Liability and testing failures | First-time shopkeepers learning the pricing loop |
| Fence | Volatile | Raids and mob justice | Risk-tolerant players chasing fast turnover |
| Hydroponic farmer | Slow build | Upfront machine and space costs | Patient players who like production chains |
| Rock bottom | Lowest floor | Everything at once | Challenge runs and second playthroughs |

Community reports consistently suggest the water and farming routes as learning shops, because the failure states are gentler: bad stock costs you money rather than a raid. If you want the fastest education in haggling and appraisal, the fence start will teach you those lessons whether you are ready or not.

**Tip:** treat your first trade as a tutorial, not a commitment. Expanding into a second product line later is cheaper than recovering from an early raid.

## Inspect, Appraise, and Price Like a Professional

Every item in your stock carries its own risk profile. A pack of cigarettes needs a visual check before resale, water needs testing and filtering, weapons need permits and modifications, and profitable chemicals depend on closely guarded recipes. Rare goods require proper appraisal, or you will overpay for something you cannot move.

| Item type | Check before selling | Cost of skipping the check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cigarette packs | Visual inspection for condition and labeling | Unsellable or discounted stock |
| Water | Test and filter every batch | Liability claims from customers |
| Weapons | Permits, modifications, and paperwork | Inspection flags and confiscation |
| Chemicals | Recipe accuracy and quality control | Addiction fallout and reputation damage |
| Rare goods | Appraisal against real market value | Overpaying on the buy side |

The golden rule from player experience: your margin is decided when you buy, not when you sell. Suppliers overstate value and contraband often hides problems you only notice after the deal closes, so budget a small loss allowance into every purchase. If you misjudge an item or the person selling it, you absorb that cost personally.

### Haggling Without Getting Burned

Haggling is where new shopkeepers win a few credits and lose a customer for life. Push too hard on a regular and you damage a relationship that was worth more than the discount. The trade-off is usually baked into the deal — a great price often means a customer who expects a favor later, or a supplier who remembers you were difficult.

### When to Sell Counterfeit or Cut Quality

Cutting quality and relabeling counterfeit goods as genuine are legitimate tools in this game, and selling fakes to the arrogant elite is one of the more profitable plays available. The catch is that reputation buys access and protection but also paints a target on your back. Reserve counterfeit sales for customers with no leverage over you, and never counterfeit for anyone who sits close to Security.

## Hide the Contraband, Then Hide It Better

Contraband is the highest-margin inventory in the game and the fastest route to a raid. Hiding stolen goods from the authorities is a core loop, and if you get caught, bribing your way out is usually cheaper than losing the whole shipment — but not always cheaper than never buying it.

- **Keep a clean front.** Stock legal goods near the counter so a spot inspection finds something ordinary first.
- **Rotate hiding spots.** Community reports suggest predictable stashes get found; treat your hiding places like inventory that needs refreshing.
- **Cap your exposure.** Never hold more contraband than one bad week of rent can absorb.
- **Watch the mob.** Security is not the only threat — mob justice arrives faster and negotiates less.

Bribes are a pressure valve, not a strategy. If you are bribing every week, your margins are already gone.

## Do the Rent Math Before You Expand

Rent keeps rising, and confiscated stock or one bad deal can leave you scrambling for the next payment. Expansion only makes sense once the current shop pays for itself with a buffer left over. The table below is a simple order of operations that community members report working well.

| Priority | Action | Why it comes at this stage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Build a two-payment rent buffer | Survives one confiscation or one dead week |
| 2 | Invest in machines and production chains | Turns bought stock into crafted, higher-value goods |
| 3 | Unlock advanced licenses | Opens the underground market and better supplier access |
| 4 | Add a second business | Diversifies income before rent scales again |
| 5 | Chase the buyout | Ends the rent clock permanently |

Every purchase needs to earn its keep. Before buying a machine, ask what it produces, how many sales that product needs to pay for the machine, and whether you can move that volume at your current reputation. If the answer to the last question is no, the machine is a decoration.

## Read the Customer, Not Just the Item

You never really know who is on the other side of the counter. Today's customer could be a security fisher collecting evidence, a revolutionary looking for leverage, or a fallen elite testing whether you are still useful. Approve, refuse, report, or look away — someone is always taking notes.

| Customer type | Warning sign | Safer response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Security fisher | Asks precise questions about stock origin | Show legal inventory, keep answers short |
| Revolutionary | Wants leverage, not product | Sell, do not promise, do not report |
| Fallen elite | Flashes status, expects deference | Sell genuine goods at full margin |
| Aug with a rogue chip | Behavioral tells that do not quite fit | Follow station policy and stay alert |

The Aug screening mechanic deserves its own note. A sentient AI chip went rogue and took its hosts with it, and Augs look like everyone else until they do not. Every faction agrees on the policy and you have the tool to enforce it, but missing the signs — or misreading them — carries its own price. Do not enforce on a hunch; a false call costs you a customer and possibly a faction's goodwill.

## FAQ

**What is the best starting trade in Probably Stolen Cyberpunk Shopkeeper Sim?**
For a first run, the water merchant or hydroponic farmer path gives you the gentlest failure states while you learn pricing and inspection. Experienced players often move to gunsmith or chemist once they understand the rent curve. Apply the same **Probably Stolen Cyberpunk Shopkeeper Sim gameplay tips** to any path: inspect before you buy, keep a rent buffer, and never hold more contraband than you can lose.

**How do I stop getting raided for contraband?**
Reduce exposure, rotate hiding spots, and keep legal stock in visible slots. Bribe your way out if you are caught, but treat repeated bribes as a sign your inventory mix is wrong.

**Should I sell counterfeit goods to the elite?**
Yes, with limits. The elite pay well and rarely inspect closely, but anyone connected to Security should get genuine goods only.

**How much should I keep in reserve for rent?**
A buffer worth two full rent payments is the community benchmark. That covers one confiscated shipment or one slow week without forcing a fire sale.

**Does the game run well on Steam Deck?**
Steam Deck compatibility is listed as Playable, and the mouse-only option plus adjustable text size make handheld sessions comfortable for stocking and haggling.
